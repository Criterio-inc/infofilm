## S01 · the-year
<!-- title: One year -->
<!-- facts: 1.1 1.2 -->
> visual: the Earth laps the Sun on a dashed orbit while a day counter runs to 365; on "almost" the decimals ,2422 snap on in red. Then "= 365 days + 5 h 49 min", and a pie with a red quarter: ≈ ¼ DAY behind every year.

Why does February sometimes have twenty-nine days? The Earth takes three hundred and sixty-five days to go once around the Sun. Almost! It actually takes about five hours and forty-nine minutes more.

Our calendar counts whole days. So every year it falls about a quarter of a day behind.

## S02 · the-rule
<!-- title: The rule -->
<!-- facts: 1.3 1.4 1.5 -->
> visual: three rule plates in a row: ÷ 4 +1 DAY, ÷ 100 NO LEAP DAY, ÷ 400 LEAP DAY AFTER ALL. Below, 1900, 2000 and 2100 land on their words, each with its verdict.

The fix? Every fourth year gets one extra day: the twenty-ninth of February. But that overshoots a little.

So century years skip it. Nineteen hundred was not a leap year. Unless the year divides by four hundred: the year two thousand was! And twenty-one hundred will not be.
